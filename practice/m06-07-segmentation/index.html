Reading any output the same way

A table of numbers tells a business nothing until someone says what it means. The product of your analysis is not "a mean of 4.21". It is one sentence a business can act on. Read every output in the same three steps.

StepAsk yourself
What it isWhat is in each row and each column? One customer, one order, one segment, one cluster? A count, an average or a share of the total?
The number that mattersWhich one or two numbers answer the question? Compare it with something: the median Recency of all customers, the share of customers against the share of spend, the cluster before it.
What it means for the businessSay it in a sentence with a group of customers in it: who they are, how many, how much money, and what the business could do.

The words in one line each

  • Recency, Frequency, Monetary (R, F, M): days since the last order, how many different orders, and total spend.
  • Score (1 to 5): each of R, F and M cut into five equal groups with pd.qcut. 5 is best. For Recency, recent is best, so its labels run backwards.
  • RFM_Score and segment: the three scores added up (3 to 15), and the label segment_customer() gives that total, from Champions (12 or more) down to Lost (3).
  • Scaling: putting every column on the same footing (mean 0, standard deviation 1) so that no column takes over the distance.
  • Inertia and the elbow: inertia is how spread out the clusters are (smaller is tighter). It always falls as k grows, so you look for where the fall slows.
  • Cluster: a group K-Means found. Its number means nothing. Name it by its profile.
  • Spend to date (CLTV here): what a customer has paid so far. It is a value so far, not a forecast.

# Task 1: Calculate RFM metrics
snapshot_date = df["InvoiceDate"].max() + pd.Timedelta(days=1)
